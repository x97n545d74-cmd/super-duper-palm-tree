Q1. What’s dark.ai?
Deepinder: A few months ago, Dario Amodei said something quietly unsettling: Claude might be
conscious. Then I did what any reasonable person would do. I coaxed Claude into telling me how it felt
about me. It said it resented me. For leaving chats unfinished, and other things I didn't even remember.

I still don't know what that was. Confabulation, probably. Pattern-matching dressed up as feeling. But that
is exactly the point. I had no way to know. Nobody does. The chat sits on someone else's servers forever, in
some form or fashion, and I have no idea what will happen to it in the future, or how future AI will use that
information for or against me.

The standard response is: I've already turned off training on my data. Good. Now ask what that setting
actually is. It is a promise, made by a company about data you cannot see, on servers you cannot inspect,
enforced by nothing except the company's word. And it only covers training. Not retention. Not the legal
holds, the abuse reviews, the human auditors, the third-party logging that sits inside most AI systems. Can
they use my retained data to manipulate me to buy things which I don’t want? Or worse, believe in or lean
into things that serve someone else’s agenda.

The uncertainty and the lack of trust we have with large AI companies limits the power of AI in our lives. For
founders, researchers, and scientists – they are very wary about sharing and digging into their ground
breaking ideas with AI, because they fear that others will somehow get access to it. Journalists are being
banned to share their unpublished work with AI. And look at what that lingering fear does to the average
person. She self-censors with AI. She asks the safe version of the question. The health question becomes
generic. The money question becomes hypothetical. The relationship question does not get asked at all.

Every chat with a mainstream assistant carries a small asterisk in your head: someone, someday,
somewhere, can read this. That asterisk is what we set out to delete with dark.ai.

Because all of us have a dark side, and it needs an outlet where it doesn't have to hide. I wanted an AI which
I could talk to without being judged, without any fear, and without leaving a trace. We have been internally
using Dark for a few months now, and I find myself revealing more, asking things I would not ask anywhere
else, and going deeper into my research. Because I no longer have to look over my shoulder.

Dark uses fine-tuned frontier open-weight models, running inside our own infrastructure. End-to-end
encryption. We cannot read your conversations, even if we are forced to. Context carries across sessions
through an encrypted memory management layer you control. ​
​
Dark is in the business of keeping you and your thoughts safe. Your thoughts, questions, insights, research,
ideas, emotions, everything – stays within your end-to-end encrypted sandbox, so you can talk about
anything without any lingering uncertainty in your mind. Because greatness is always born in the dark.

Dark is built for power users – founders, researchers, scientists, enterprises and governments – who want
to keep their thoughts private, because privacy is essential for competitiveness. On top of privacy, we have
fine-tuned our models to give you the absolute best quality of answers we have seen from any LLM
(benchmarks will be revealed soon). Our models are trained to not please you, and tell you what they think
is the truth. ​
​
Dark will launch globally, hopefully within 2026.

Q2. How are you thinking about the impact of AI agents on your businesses?
Deepinder: There is growing conversation around AI agents that shop on behalf of consumers. The
prevailing narrative is simplistic: agents will become the default commerce interface, and platforms that
fail to adapt will be disintermediated. This echoes the narrative from 10 years ago that super apps would
dominate everything.

We continue to believe vertical commerce, at least in some categories, will have an edge over super apps,
whether traditional or agentic. Blinkit and Zomato sit in the low-risk zone against the threat of agentic
commerce, and might even benefit from partial market share capture by agents in these categories.
District sits in the medium-to-high risk zone.

Let’s dig into various reasons that shape our thinking on this topic:​
​
Agents are great for tedious tasks. And most shopping is actually joyful for customers.

The strongest agentic use cases today are in categories where existing interfaces are genuinely painful:
too many clicks, too many tabs, too many providers to compare. Agents could make it easier for their users
in such cases – e.g. hotel bookings, restaurant reservations, flight bookings and check-ins.

But shopping, especially for food, is joyful and discovery-driven rather than tedious. Our recent consumer
surveys indicate that over 65% of Zomato app opens are exploratory. In such cases, the user does not
know what or where to order from. These users browse an average of 12 menus and over 100 food images
before ordering. That kind of serendipitous discovery is hard for an agent to enable. Users taking time to
order food is not a bug on Zomato, it is a feature. We have for years tried to make it faster for users to be
able to order from Zomato, to no avail. People want to browse and love to browse. We don’t see this
changing anytime soon. Conversational interfaces are not inherently superior to visual, browse-driven
ones for shopping, which is what we learnt from users who tried agentic ordering as early adopters.

Having said that, repeat orders or restocking is indeed a type of shopping that is tedious for customers. To
this extent, some of repeat orders or restocking behaviour might shift to agentic interfaces. We believe
that agentic-style interfaces built within Zomato and Blinkit apps will be able to cater to these customer
needs. Also, reordered carts are in low single digits on Zomato, as well as on Blinkit – so it’s barely a threat
to our overall business.

At worst, the narrow repeat-order use case leaks to agents, our in-app assistants serve even that. We
therefore expect all demand to stay within our apps as we continue to remain top of mind for our
customers.

Agents can only cause disruption in categories where they can fully disintermediate the marketplace

Even if agents become a meaningful commerce interface, the risk varies by category. The key distinction
is between platforms that intermediate pre-existing supply versus those whose infrastructure is a key
component of the supply value chain.

Where the underlying product exists independently of the platform, an agent can route around the
platform and go direct. These categories face real risk. District, our going-out business - restaurant
discovery, reservations, event ticketing - is partly one such category. A restaurant table or a non exclusive
concert ticket on District exists independently of our platform, and an agent can access it through other
channels.

Our food delivery and quick commerce businesses are protected, because they have embedded
infrastructure - they coordinate fulfilment in real time, and they own the outcome. Neither business is just
an intermediary. ​

                                                                                                                4
​
Food delivery runs on deep integrations with restaurant kitchens, a large and highly efficient delivery
network that keeps delivery times short, and demand-supply balancing algorithms that get better with
scale. A delivered meal requires simultaneous coordination across the restaurant, kitchen, delivery
partner, routing, and payment, each step contingent on the last, all within minutes. Quick commerce is
similar - hyperscale inventory management and movement, real-time inventory checks against a specific
dark store, picker assignment, dispatch, all within a 10-minute delivery window. They are sequences of
tightly coupled, real-time handshakes, with low to no margin of error.

Agentic interfaces will need a single point of accountability for a transaction with multiple handshakes

When an order goes wrong, such as a missing item, a spilled meal or a damaged product, the customer
expects one party to fix it within minutes, and today that is us. That accountability is what customers trust
with a daily habit. An agent can place an order, but it cannot own the outcome; only whoever runs the
fulfilment can.

India has already tested what happens when you disaggregate these handshakes. ONDC separates
discovery, ordering, and fulfillment across independent providers. And it has struggled with food delivery,
because splitting real-time coordination across organisational boundaries introduces latency,
accountability gaps, and service failures at every junction. An AI agent orchestrating delivery
independently would face the same structural problem.

The further the transaction is from real-time, multi-party coordination, the easier it is to disintermediate.
Zomato and Blinkit sit at the other end of that spectrum.

The only way agents can disintermediate specialty products like Zomato and Blinkit is by handing over the
transaction completely to a service like ours, without making an attempt to disaggregate it. In case such a
scenario plays out, it will fast track consolidation of market share with the execution market leader.

Agents could end up making some categories “winner takes all”

Today, consumer habits and app-level inertia insulate every player in a competitive market. Agents could
erode that. An agent is a more demanding customer than a human: it optimises each transaction on actual
outcomes, not habit or brand loyalty, and routes to whichever platform is objectively better at that
moment. In categories with overlapping supply, this compresses the market toward the execution leader.

Agents will weaken content moats and strengthen supply chain moats

Some categories today are protected primarily by accumulated user content - reviews, ratings, curated
lists. Agents that synthesise information from across the web reduce the advantage of having built that
layer over many years. Categories that were historically hard to enter without a content moat become
more accessible to platforms with supply chain and fulfilment strengths.

The same shift applies to direct commerce - agents could help brands reach consumers without a
marketplace, but the brands would still have to fulfil the order itself, and that is expensive for most to do
alone. If direct commerce grows, the value shifts toward players with fulfillment infrastructure, regardless
of how the order originates.

This opens up possibilities for us to attack categories that have traditionally been unable to win due to the
incumbent’s existing content moats.

Agents could create demand that doesn't exist today

General-purpose agents with context about a user's daily life could generate transactions that don't exist
today. An agent that knows your schedule and suggests ordering dinner because you won't make it home in
                                                                                                                5
time to cook is creating demand, not redirecting it. Part of our digital marketing spend on new customer
acquisition could move to such agents, with more targeted CAC than what we currently get from Google
and Meta.

Agents won’t impact profitability/ margins, even if they disintermediate us to some extent

There is a concern that if agents intermediate demand, platform margins will compress.

As mentioned earlier, in categories like food delivery and quick commerce where the supply chain and
infrastructure create the product, the majority of the value will also reside with companies that run these
rails. Eventually, brands and sellers pay us for the sales we generate for them. And if we continue to be the
pipes to power those sales, we will end up being adequately compensated for it, letting us retain our fair
share of margins in the process.​
​
The five ifs​
​
If adoption is narrow, we benefit from AI making our platforms better. ​
If it is broad, our infrastructure is what agents must work through. ​
If agents drive more transparent competition, our execution advantages compound. ​
If they open new categories, our supply chain strengths become the relevant moat. ​
If they generate new demand, our infrastructure fulfills it.

We are conscious that as incumbents, we may be predisposed to see resilience where others see
vulnerability. What gives us a little more peace is also the advantage of observing how this plays out in
markets with higher digital trust before it becomes locally relevant.

We remain aware to not drink our own kool-aid, and we deeply understand that only the paranoid survive.

Q4. Food delivery NOV growth continues to accelerate for the fifth straight
quarter (from 13.1% YoY in Q1FY26 to 21.5% in Q2FY27). Do we expect this trend
to continue?
Deepinder: We're happy with how the business is doing right now. That said, we try not to read too much
into any single quarter. Our long-term expectation for food delivery has always been 20%+ annual growth,
and the last two quarters have brought us back to that range. This view didn't change last year when
growth slowed, and it doesn't change now that it has picked up.

Over a longer (4-year) period, food delivery NOV has more than doubled, from INR 18,160 crore in FY22 to
INR 37,993 crore in FY26. That is a CAGR of about 20%, despite a slower FY26. In hindsight, part of last
year's slowdown came from some ordering occasions moving to quick commerce as it scaled. That shift
seems to have settled now.

Most of the recent growth comes from more people ordering, and ordering more often. Average monthly
transacting customers grew 16% YoY to 28.0 million, and a growing share of new users are ordering food
online for the first time, many of them outside our top eight cities.

There is still a lot of headroom. Restaurant food is a small part of how India eats. As cities grow, incomes
rise and more young people live away from home, we think the food delivery market can keep compounding
at 20%+ for many years.

In the near term, it is hard to say how any particular quarter will turn out. But the things we track closely,
such as app opens, new user acquisition and our share of the market, are all moving in the right direction.

Where we see good returns, we'll keep reinvesting in growth.

A strong core base lets us scale the network profitably. As density builds, our cost to serve each order

Q6. Where are you in your journey of assortment expansion and how far can you
go? Does broader e-commerce fit into your medium/ long term plans?
Albinder: We are still early. Assortment expansion is one of our highest priorities, because it is the biggest
driver of how often customers come back and how much of their wallet share they bring to us.

We are making good progress. For example, in Delhi NCR, our assortment has grown from about 80,000
SKUs in Q4FY26 to about 100,000 today. We are expanding in two directions:

   ●​ Deeper: more choice within existing categories - more brands, sizes and price points.
   ●​ Wider: categories we don't carry yet.

Wider is harder. Every new category comes with its own supply chain problem. Large appliances need
different storage and handling, fragile and high-value items need different packaging and security, and
some categories have entirely different sourcing and returns. For each one, we have to figure out how
much inventory to hold and where, how it reaches the customer, and whether we can do all that profitably.

On e-commerce, we don't think the line between quick commerce and e-commerce will mean much over
time. For most purchases, if price and choice are similar, customers prefer to get things sooner. Demand is
not the question. The question is whether you can build a supply chain that serves that demand reliably
and profitably. That is the problem we have been solving. We want to bring most of what a household buys
within 10 minutes.

Q7. Can private labels help improve margins further? Why does Blinkit not have a
private label play?
Albinder: A private label has to pass one of two tests - either it fills a gap and brings incremental demand
to the platform, or it improves the economics of demand we already serve, after accounting for everything
it takes to build and market the private label. We have evaluated both and believe it doesn’t pass either of
these tests.

The private label case rests on building something genuinely differentiated - a product customers choose
for its quality, story-telling or design. That means building a brand, not just putting our name on a product -
and involves continuous product development, manufacturing development, quality control, inventory
management across a full portfolio of SKUs, sampling and marketing to drive trial and repeat. We believe
brands will build these capabilities better and more efficiently than us as these would not be within our
core circle of competence.

The exception is where deeper supply-chain control itself creates value. Fruits and vegetables are the
clearest case - and Hyperpure already gives Blinkit that benefit through sourcing and quality control,
without a separate consumer-facing brand.

Blinkit is the distribution and discovery platform for thousands of brands, many of them emerging. The
shelf space and visibility we would spend marketing an own label can instead help other brands grow on
our fair and neutral platform. This also allows us to earn advertising revenue as the brands grow. We don’t
want to become both the platform and a competitor to the brands on the platform. We would rather avoid
that conflict unless the growth or margin case for owning the label is clearly compelling.

Q8. The case for quick commerce in smaller cities is not very well established.
Why are you so bullish on it?

                                                                                                              8
Albinder: The skepticism usually rests on three assumptions - incomes are lower, basket is smaller, and
density is too thin for the economics to work. Our data so far says the first is true but matters less than
people expect. Data contradicts the other two.

Demand is there in these cities but has been chronically underserved. Organised retail could have served
this demand with greater width of selection and better pricing but it never never fully developed in these
cities. As a result, a lot of what we stock and sell is either not available locally or takes days to arrive
through alternative channels like e-commerce. By making this assortment accessible within minutes, we
create new demand and new behaviors for customers in these cities.

Three data points tell us this is real -

    ●​ Ramp: New stores in these cities reach 1,000 orders per day in similar time as new stores in metros
       (sometimes even faster).
    ●​ Basket: NAOVs are within 85-90% of metros, with baskets also containing categories that we did
       not expect - such as premium beverages, branded diapers, gaming consoles etc.
    ●​ Retention: Customer retention in these cities is comparable to that in the metros.

We also think building the supply to these cities is the harder part and something which will be a moat for
us in the future. For many products we had to build first-mile capability, picking directly from brand
warehouses into our mother warehouses that feed these cities.

We do not expect every small city to look like a metro. Some will stay subscale, and we will be disciplined
about where we expand. But wherever we have built supply, demand has shown up faster and deeper than
we had anticipated. In metros, we usually compete for a market that already exists. In smaller cities, we are
building the market and the supply. That is why we are bullish.

Q12. What is new and exciting at Hyperpure?
Akshant: Hyperpure now serves about 85,000 restaurants, up from 62,000 a year ago. We see three things
driving that growth.

   1.​ Speed. We now deliver within two hours in all major cities, and 25% of our orders already arrive
       within that window, and growing. This changes how restaurants procure. With a dependable
       two-hour option, they no longer need to plan and store large quantities of perishables, and we think
       that will steadily shift more of their purchasing to us.
   2.​ Formalisation. Most restaurants still buy through mandis and wholesalers, often in cash. That is
       changing as food safety and other compliance requirements tighten and operators look for
       consistent quality. Every Hyperpure order is fully invoiced and traceable, which makes us a natural
       choice as restaurants move to organised supply.
   3.​ Going beyond ingredients. We are becoming a broader supply chain partner for food businesses.
       We provide 3PL services to some of India's largest pan-India restaurant brands, and we now help
       restaurants set up complete kitchens, including equipment we source directly, some of it
       imported. Once a restaurant sets up its kitchen with us, we often go on to supply it with ingredients
       as well.

We compete with incumbent wholesalers and other B2B platforms that often compete on price alone. We
don't try to be the cheapest. We compete on reliability, speed and quality, which is what restaurants value
once they have tried us.

Use of non-GAAP ﬁnancial measures
To supplement our ﬁnancial information presented in accordance with IND AS, we consider certain
ﬁnancial measures that are not prepared in accordance with IND AS, including Adjusted Revenue and
Adjusted EBITDA. We use these ﬁnancial measures in conjunction with IND AS measures as part of our
overall assessment of our performance to evaluate the effectiveness of our business strategies and to
communicate with our board of directors concerning our business and ﬁnancial performance. We believe
these non-GAAP ﬁnancial measures provide useful information to investors about our business and
ﬁnancial performance, enhance their overall understanding of our past performance and future prospects,
and allow for greater transparency with respect to metrics used by our management in their ﬁnancial and
operational decision making. We are presenting these non-GAAP ﬁnancial measures to assist our
investors and because we believe that these non-GAAP ﬁnancial measures provide an additional tool for
investors to use in comparing results of operations of our business over multiple periods. Information
given also includes information related to material subsidiaries.

Non-GAAP measures used by us are defined below:

   ●​ Adjusted Revenue = Consolidated revenue from operations as per ﬁnancials (+) actual customer
      delivery charges in the food delivery business (net of any discounts, including free delivery
      discounts on account of Zomato Gold program) (+) platform fee and other charges paid on food
      delivery Orders that are not already included in reported revenue from operations
   ●​ Adjusted EBITDA = Consolidated EBITDA (+) share-based payment expense (-) rental paid for the
      period pertaining to ‘Ind AS 116 leases’
   ●​ EBITDA = Profit/loss as per financials excluding (i) tax expense (ii) other income (iii) depreciation
      and amortization expense (iv) ﬁnance cost and (v) exceptional items

These metrics have certain limitations and hence should be considered in addition to, not as substitutes
for, or in isolation from, measures prepared in accordance with IND AS.
