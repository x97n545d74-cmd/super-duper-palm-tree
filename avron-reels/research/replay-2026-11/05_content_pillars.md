# Module 5: Content pillars for the "how India speaks" page

Project Replay, module 5. Written 2026-11-06. Builds on Module 1 (sections 1 and 4), Module 2 (sections C, D and E) and Module 3 (sections 6 and 7). Brand decisions live in `09_brand.md`; this file names the working name Bolchaal where a page name is needed, with Kya Kehte and Wordmala as fallbacks.

Labels: [FACT] for a sourced finding, [INTERPRETATION] for my reading of it, [HYPOTHESIS] for an untested claim.

## 1. How the pillars were chosen

Module 3 section 7.1 gives the niche eight reasons to win: sends, replay, comments, cross-region belonging, supply, weak competition, feasibility and longevity. Module 1 section 1 says the three ranking signals that matter are watch time, likes and sends, and that sends and rewatches have separate causes. Module 2 section C says the goal is viewing types 3 to 6 (rewatch to understand, for enjoyment, for a hidden detail, because the ending changes the beginning) and that type 3 is the one to guard against.

So each pillar below is built around one engagement objective and one replay device from Module 2 section D, not a mix. A pillar that tries to earn sends, saves and comments at once earns none of them well. [INTERPRETATION]

The eight pillars, one line each:

| # | Pillar | Series name | Purpose | One metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | What do you call it maps | Kya Kehte | Share, community identity | Sends per reach |
| 2 | Word journeys (origins and loanwords) | Safar | Replay | Plays per reach |
| 3 | Place-name meanings | Naam | Save | Saves per reach |
| 4 | Scripts and letters | Lipi | Authority, awe | Follows per reach |
| 5 | City slang | Sheher Ki Boli | Emotional connection, belonging | Comments per reach |
| 6 | Indian English | Angrezi, Indian Style | Anticipation for the next episode | Follower return rate |
| 7 | Community reel | Aapne Bola | Community identity | Comment-to-follow conversion |
| 8 | Hidden-detail puzzle (experimental) | Dhoondo | Replay, pure test of the thesis | Plays per reach |

Metrics are defined in Module 2 section F2. "Per reach" means divided by accounts reached, so that a reel with a big reach does not look better than it is.

## 2. The pillars

### Pillar 1: Kya Kehte (what do you call it)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Psychological purpose | Share and community identity. The send is the content: "this is what we call it" goes to one friend from another state (Module 3, 7.1, reason 1). |
| Target audience | 18 to 34, bilingual Hinglish plus a home language, all regions equally. Second ring: 35+ parents via WhatsApp forwards (Module 1, 4.2). |
| Viewer motivation | Find my state, then check whether the page got it right. |
| Emotional trigger | Recognition, then mild pride or mild outrage ("we do not say that"). High-arousal, which Module 1 section 1 point 4 says drives sharing. |
| Engagement objective | Sends per reach. |
| Storytelling format | One object, one question, twelve to fifteen answers, revealed one state per beat. The object comes first as a photo or illustration with no label, so the viewer names it silently before the map starts. |
| Visual language | The map fill (brand move 1 in `09_brand.md`): India outline on paper, one state fills per beat, label snaps in, the bindu dot lands on the state. Six fills at most, grouped by cognate family, labels do the work. |
| Episode template | 0 to 2 s: the object, no words. 2 to 4 s: "Kya kehte?" title. 4 to 22 s: the fill, one state per 1.2 s, cognate families fill together. 22 to 26 s: the one word the page did not expect (the outlier), held. 26 to 30 s: end card "Aapke yahan?" (what about where you are). |
| Lengths to test | 22 s and 34 s. [HYPOTHESIS: the shorter one wins on sends, the longer on comments] |
| Frequency | Weekly, Monday. |
| CTA approach | The end card asks "Aapke yahan?" and shows a blank map tile. No instruction to comment. The gap does the asking. |
| Replay device | Hidden detail (D2): the viewer's state appears for one beat; the second watch is to find it and to read the rest. Plus a loop seam: the end card's blank tile is the opening object's frame, so the reel returns to the object. |
| Accuracy rule | Every word on the map must appear in a dictionary of that language or in the PLSI volume for that state, with the source line on screen. Where usage is split inside a state, show two labels, never pick one. Words that are offensive in another language get a one-line note, never a joke. |
| Example titles | "Bhindi. Vendakkai. Bhendi. Okra: fifteen names, one vegetable." / "What you call your mother's brother, state by state." / "Brinjal, baingan, kathirikai, vankaya: the aubergine map." |

### Pillar 2: Safar (word journeys)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Psychological purpose | Replay. The ending reframes the opening: once you know "shampoo" is "champo", you want to see the first frame again (Module 2, D1 reframe loop). |
| Target audience | 20 to 34, English-metro and Tier 2 graduates, the group most likely to use the English word daily. Diaspora as a third ring (Module 3, 7.1). |
| Viewer motivation | "I have said this word my whole life and never knew." |
| Emotional trigger | Awe at scale (a word that crossed three empires) and ownership ("that word is ours"). |
| Engagement objective | Plays per reach (Module 2, F2). |
| Storytelling format | A word travels. Open on the word as the viewer knows it, in the setting where they use it (a bottle, a menu, a train ticket). Trace it back one hop at a time, with a date and a place per hop. Close on the first frame with one letter or one word changed. |
| Visual language | The travelling word (brand move 2): the word slides along a route on the map or a timeline, and its letters swap one at a time as it changes language. Dictionary entry card for each hop. |
| Episode template | 0 to 3 s: the familiar object with the word on it. 3 to 6 s: "This word is not from here" (or "This word is from here"). 6 to 30 s: three to five hops, each a card with place, year, source. 30 to 36 s: the first frame again, one word swapped. Last VO line is the first VO line with one word changed (D1 sentence loop). |
| Lengths to test | 30 s and 45 s. [HYPOTHESIS: 45 s wins on plays per reach only if the hops are five, not three] |
| Frequency | Twice a week, Tuesday and Saturday (Saturday is the ledger form, see below). |
| CTA approach | None spoken. The end frame carries the source line and the page mark. Curiosity is the CTA: the next hop is promised in the caption ("Next Safar: where 'loot' went"). |
| Replay device | Ending reframes opening (D1), with a sentence loop. One hidden detail allowed: the date on the first object is the real date of the first attested use. |
| Accuracy rule | Each hop needs one of the sources in section 4 and the year of first attestation where the source gives one. "Probably" and "disputed" must be said out loud when the source says so. No folk etymologies, even famous ones, unless the reel is about the folk etymology being wrong. |
| Example titles | "Shampoo: champo, a London bathhouse, and a bottle in Bareilly." / "Catamaran is Tamil. Here is the boat it names." / "Chai and tea are one word on two trade routes." |

Saturday variant, the ledger: "Ten English words India gave the world", counted up like a receipt. Same sources, same travelling word, but the counter format gives the viewer a total to check on rewatch (Module 3 sample week, Saturday).

### Pillar 3: Naam (place-name meanings)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Psychological purpose | Save. A card per city is reference material people keep and pause on. |
| Target audience | 18 to 34, with a strong second ring of 35+ who grew up in or moved from the cities named. Tier 2 cities over-indexed on purpose: a reel about a metro is expected, a reel about Bhagalpur is a gift. |
| Viewer motivation | "What does my city's name mean?" and "I can finally explain it to someone." |
| Emotional trigger | Belonging to a place, and the quiet pride of knowing its story. Collective nostalgia (Module 1, 4.6). |
| Engagement objective | Saves per reach. |
| Storytelling format | One suffix or one theme, six cities, one card each, built for pausing. The sixth card is always from the Northeast, Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh, Goa, or the islands, scheduled in, not added when someone complains. |
| Visual language | Dictionary entry cards on paper, each with headword, meaning, language of origin, source. The map shows all six as bindu dots before the cards start, so the viewer can see the spread. |
| Episode template | 0 to 2 s: "Six cities end in -abad. Here is why." 2 to 4 s: six dots on the map. 4 to 28 s: six cards, 4 s each, no transition tricks, a page-turn foley per card. 28 to 32 s: the six names in a column, the shared suffix highlighted, the source list. |
| Lengths to test | 32 s and 50 s (ten cards). [HYPOTHESIS: saves rise with card count up to about ten, then retention collapses] |
| Frequency | Weekly, Wednesday. |
| CTA approach | The end column is a reference table. People save tables. The caption adds "Your city not here? Next Naam does -pur." |
| Replay device | None by design. Saves, not replays, are the objective, and a pause-and-read reel should not be forced into a loop (Module 2, E2 point 8 deducts for loops that are the only device on long reels). |
| Accuracy rule | A place name claim needs a gazetteer, a district handbook, the Survey of India or Census spelling, and an etymology source. Where the origin is contested (most are), show both readings and name the dispute. Never state a renaming as a meaning. |
| Example titles | "-abad: who named these six cities, and when." / "Every -ur in Tamil Nadu is a village. Every -ur in Karnataka is a town. Why?" / "Thiruvananthapuram in three words." |

### Pillar 4: Lipi (scripts and letters)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Psychological purpose | Authority and awe. This pillar earns the page its right to be believed. It is also where scripts appear on screen as the point, per the editorial rule in `09_brand.md`. |
| Target audience | 22 to 40, readers in any script, people who type in Roman script but read their home script. Teachers and students as a vocal minority. |
| Viewer motivation | "Why does my script look the way it does?" |
| Emotional trigger | Awe at a 2,000-year chain of cause and effect; the pleasure of seeing a letter drawn. |
| Engagement objective | Follows per reach. |
| Storytelling format | One mechanical fact about a script, shown, not told. Why Telugu and Kannada letters are round (palm leaves split along straight lines, Module 3 sample week). Why Devanagari has a headline. How Brahmi became ten scripts. The letter is drawn on screen in the order a hand would draw it. |
| Visual language | Ink on paper. A stroke animation of one letter, large, slow. Brahmi-to-modern comparison strips. A short seamless loop of the stroke under a longer VO. |
| Episode template | 0 to 3 s: one letter drawn, no words. 3 to 6 s: the claim ("These letters are round because of a leaf"). 6 to 24 s: the mechanism, two or three beats. 24 to 30 s: the same letter drawn again, now with the viewer knowing why. |
| Lengths to test | 30 s and 40 s. |
| Frequency | Weekly, Thursday, alternating with Pillar 8 in weeks 1 to 8 of the puzzle trial. |
| Replay device | Seamless loop (D1 frame loop): an 8 s stroke loop under the VO, so the last frame is the first. The eye rewatches the drawing while the ear catches what it missed. |
| CTA approach | The page mark and series name appear on the stroke loop. The caption states what the page is ("one script fact a week") and names next week's script. That is the follow prompt. |
| Accuracy rule | Script history claims must cite a palaeography source (Salomon, Indian Epigraphy, 1998; Dani, Indian Palaeography) or a university press history of the script. The palm leaf explanation for round scripts is widely repeated and plausible but not proven; the reel says "the usual explanation" and shows why it is plausible. [FACT on the state of evidence: the palm-leaf account is a standard explanation in handbooks, not a settled finding] |
| Example titles | "Why Telugu and Kannada letters are round." / "Brahmi to Bengali in eight letters." / "The headline: why Hindi words hang from a line and Tamil words do not." |

### Pillar 5: Sheher Ki Boli (city slang)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Psychological purpose | Emotional connection and belonging. Slang is the most personal layer of language; the viewer hears their city and feels seen. |
| Target audience | 16 to 28, city-proud, meme-literate, the group that lives in the Reels tab and sends in DMs (Module 1, 4.2). |
| Viewer motivation | "Does the page know my city properly?" and "I must send this to my college group." |
| Emotional trigger | Recognition and amusement. Humour by region per Module 1 section 4.7: wordplay in Tamil, dry situational in Malayalam, loud and warm in Punjabi, self-deprecating in Hinglish. The register changes with the city; the look does not. |
| Engagement objective | Comments per reach. |
| Storytelling format | Five words from one city, each with the situation it is used in, told as a tiny scene in motion type, no actors. The fifth word is one the page admits it is not sure about, with "correct us". |
| Visual language | Dictionary entry cards, but the "meaning" line is a situation, not a definition. City map tile as the header. Roman script for all slang, with the home script shown once per reel in the header. |
| Episode template | 0 to 2 s: the city name and one word, no explanation. 2 to 26 s: five words, 5 s each, situation first, word second. 26 to 30 s: the fifth word's "we are not sure" card. |
| Lengths to test | 30 s and 20 s (three words). |
| Frequency | Weekly, Friday. |
| CTA approach | The uncertainty card is the CTA. People correct more readily than they praise. The best correction becomes a credit in Sunday's reel (Pillar 7). |
| Replay device | Audio motif: each word has a short foley tag (the bindu click), and the five tags make a rhythm that the viewer feels the second time. Visual twin: the five cards stack into a column at the end. |
| Accuracy rule | Slang has no dictionary. Rule: a word needs two independent attestations (a published glossary, a newspaper feature, a published novel or film script, or a linguistics paper on that city's speech) or it goes in the "not sure" slot. Never present caste, religion or community slurs as slang. Never mock an accent. |
| Example titles | "Five words only Mumbai says." / "Bengaluru's 'one minute' is not one minute. Five words." / "Hyderabad: 'hau', 'kaiku', 'nakko'. What they mean and when you say them." |

### Pillar 6: Angrezi, Indian Style (Indian English)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Psychological purpose | Anticipation for the next episode. A numbered series ("Indian English, word 14") that people come back for. |
| Target audience | 22 to 40, office workers, the "do the needful" generation and the one that mocks it. Diaspora strongly. |
| Viewer motivation | "Is that word actually wrong?" (usually it is not, and that is the reel). |
| Emotional trigger | Vindication. Most Indian English forms have a history and a logic; the viewer who was corrected by a boss gets to feel right. |
| Engagement objective | Follower return rate (share of views from followers, Module 2, F2). |
| Storytelling format | One word or phrase per episode. Open on the sentence as it is said in an Indian office. Show the British or American "standard". Then the history: where the Indian form comes from, often older English, Portuguese, or a translation of a Hindi, Tamil or Bengali structure. Close with the verdict: "not wrong, Indian". |
| Visual language | Dictionary entry card with two columns (India / elsewhere). The travelling word move when the form has an origin outside India. |
| Episode template | 0 to 2 s: the sentence in an office setting (text only). 2 to 5 s: "Is 'prepone' a word?" 5 to 22 s: the history, two or three hops. 22 to 28 s: verdict card, with the number of the episode, and "Next: 'pass out'". |
| Lengths to test | 28 s and 40 s. |
| Frequency | Weekly, Saturday, alternating with the Safar ledger: ledger on even weeks, Angrezi on odd weeks. [HYPOTHESIS: alternating keeps both fresh; if Angrezi outperforms by 30 percent on return rate for four weeks, it takes Saturday outright] |
| CTA approach | The episode number and the "Next:" line. Anticipation is the CTA. |
| Replay device | Callback (D5): the office sentence returns at the end with the verdict stamped on it. |
| Accuracy rule | Oxford English Dictionary or Merriam-Webster for the "standard" form and first attestation; Hobson-Jobson, Nihalani, Tongue and Hosali (Indian and British English, 2004) and Sailaja (Indian English, 2009) for the Indian form. Never say Indian English is wrong; never say British English is wrong. Say "different, and here is why". |
| Example titles | "Prepone: not in the dictionary, until it was." / "'Do the needful' is 200 years old. Here is the first letter that used it." / "Why Indians 'pass out' of college." |

### Pillar 7: Aapne Bola (the community reel)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Psychological purpose | Community identity. The page reads its comments back, maps them, and credits the people who corrected it. This is what turns viewers into members (Module 1, 3.4 on identity and social currency). |
| Target audience | Existing followers first; this reel is for the people who commented, and for the people who want to be credited next week. |
| Viewer motivation | "Did they use my comment?" |
| Emotional trigger | Being seen and named. Social currency. |
| Engagement objective | Comment-to-follow conversion (follows from the profile within 48 hours of commenting, measured by hand on a sample; Instagram Insights does not report this directly, Module 2, B5). |
| Storytelling format | The week's Kya Kehte or Sheher Ki Boli, redrawn with what the comments added. Three corrections, each with the commenter's first name or handle (with permission in the comment thread), and one "still open" question. |
| Visual language | The same map fill, but the new labels arrive in the accent colour. A credits column at the end in the dictionary card style. |
| Episode template | 0 to 3 s: "Monday we asked. You answered." 3 to 20 s: the updated map or list. 20 to 28 s: the three credits. 28 to 32 s: the open question for next week. |
| Lengths to test | 32 s and 20 s. |
| Frequency | Weekly, Sunday. |
| CTA approach | The credit is the CTA. Nobody is asked to do anything; they see that doing it is noticed. |
| Replay device | Callback (D5) to Monday's reel, which is the one the viewer already watched; the second watch is of the original. |
| Accuracy rule | A comment is not a source. A correction is used when a source in section 4 supports it, or when it is reported as "three people from Dharwad say", with the number and the place, not as fact. |
| Example titles | "You fixed our bhindi map. Here is version two." / "Forty-one people from Kerala said the same thing. They were right." / "The word we got wrong in Hyderabad, and who caught it." |

### Pillar 8: Dhoondo (hidden-detail puzzle, experimental)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Psychological purpose | Replay, and nothing else. Module 3 section 7.3 calls this the purest test of the thesis: if a 12 s puzzle reel earns two to three plays per reach, the mechanism is proven and can be lifted into the other pillars. |
| Target audience | 16 to 28, any language, because the puzzle needs almost no text. |
| Viewer motivation | "I can find it." |
| Emotional trigger | Challenge, then satisfaction. |
| Engagement objective | Plays per reach. |
| Storytelling format | One frame, one task. "One of these twelve words is not Sanskrit." "One state on this map has the wrong name." "Find the English word in this Tamil sentence." The answer is in the frame from the start, legible, and is revealed in the last second. |
| Visual language | The map fill or the entry card, with no VO or a one-line VO. The answer reveal uses the bindu dot. |
| Episode template | 0 to 1 s: the task. 1 to 10 s: the frame, held, with a slow push. 10 to 12 s: the bindu lands on the answer. Loop. |
| Lengths to test | 12 s and 8 s. |
| Frequency | Thursday, alternating with Lipi, for eight weeks. Then decide: keep, fold into Pillar 1, or drop (Module 3, 7.3). |
| CTA approach | None. The caption carries the answer and the source, so nobody has to ask. |
| Replay device | Hidden detail (D2) plus a frame loop (D1), the two devices Module 2 says work best under 10 to 15 s. |
| Accuracy rule | The puzzle's premise must be true and sourced like any other claim. "Not Sanskrit" means a dictionary gives a non-Sanskrit origin, and the caption cites it. No trick questions that rest on a disputed etymology. |
| Example titles | "One of these twelve words is Portuguese. Find it." / "One state on this map is spelt the pre-2011 way." / "Three Tamil words. One is English in disguise." |

## 3. The weekly mix

| Day | Pillar | Series | Why this slot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | 1 | Kya Kehte | The highest-send format opens the week and seeds the comments that Sunday's reel needs. Monday evening is when college and office groups are most active after the weekend. [HYPOTHESIS] |
| Tuesday | 2 | Safar | The replay format follows the share format, so the new viewers Monday brought in meet the page's best rewatch piece on their second day. |
| Wednesday | 3 | Naam | Mid-week is the save slot: a reference piece for people who will come back to it. It also gives the week's quiet, long-retention reel. |
| Thursday | 4 or 8 | Lipi / Dhoondo | Lipi earns authority; Dhoondo tests replay. Alternating keeps Thursday the "page does something different" day. After week 8, one of them takes the slot. |
| Friday | 5 | Sheher Ki Boli | The comment engine runs on Friday night, when the 16 to 28 audience is in group chats. Comments gathered Friday to Sunday morning feed Sunday's reel. |
| Saturday | 2 (ledger) or 6 | Safar ledger / Angrezi | The weekend slot alternates two series with a counting or numbering device, both built for "which one did I miss" rewatching. |
| Sunday | 7 | Aapne Bola | Closes the loop. Credits Friday's and Monday's commenters. Posted Sunday evening, when family groups are most active and parents forward (Module 1, 4.3). |

Two rules on the order. First, a share reel is never followed by another share reel: Monday's sends bring strangers, Tuesday's replay keeps them. Second, the community reel always comes last, so there is a week of material to credit.

Festival and event weeks replace one slot, never the whole week: a Diwali Kya Kehte ("what do you call the lamp"), a Pongal Naam, an IPL Sheher Ki Boli ("five words every stadium in India shouts"). The calendar in Module 1 section 4.5 applies.

## 4. Sourcing policy

The page's claim to exist is that it is right. Module 1 section 4.3 says the Indian forward environment is high-volume and low-trust, and that a consistently sourced page becomes the thing people forward when they want to be believed. Every claim on screen carries a source line. These are the sources that count.

### 4.1 Acceptable references

| Claim type | Acceptable sources | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| English etymology, first attestation | Oxford English Dictionary (oed.com, subscription); Merriam-Webster (merriam-webster.com); Etymonline (etymonline.com) | Etymonline alone is enough for a caption; a reel claim needs OED or Merriam-Webster as well where the date matters. |
| Indian words in English | Hobson-Jobson (Yule and Burnell, 1903 ed., on DSAL); OED; Nihalani, Tongue and Hosali, Indian and British English (OUP, 2004); Sailaja, Indian English (EUP, 2009) | Hobson-Jobson is a colonial text; use it for attestations and quotes, and say so when its framing is dated. |
| Hindi and Urdu | Platts, A Dictionary of Urdu, Classical Hindi and English (1884, on DSAL); McGregor, Oxford Hindi-English Dictionary (1993, on DSAL); Rekhta Dictionary (rekhtadictionary.com) for Urdu; Bahri or Hindi Shabdsagar for Hindi | Rekhta is good for meaning and usage, weaker on etymology. Shabdkosh.com and HinKhoj are not sources for origin claims. |
| Sanskrit and Prakrit | Monier-Williams Sanskrit-English Dictionary (1899); Turner, A Comparative Dictionary of the Indo-Aryan Languages (1966, on DSAL); Mayrhofer EWAia for deep etymology | Turner is the backbone for any "Sanskrit to modern language" chain. |
| Tamil | Tamil Lexicon (University of Madras, 1924 to 1936, on DSAL); Burrow and Emeneau, A Dravidian Etymological Dictionary (2nd ed. 1984, DEDR, on DSAL); Cre-A Dictionary of Contemporary Tamil for modern usage | DEDR numbers go on screen where a Dravidian cognate set is the claim. |
| Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Tulu | DEDR; Brown's Telugu-English Dictionary; Kittel's Kannada-English Dictionary; Gundert's Malayalam-English Dictionary (all on DSAL); Manner's Tulu-English Dictionary | Kittel and Gundert are nineteenth-century; use them for attestation and confirm modern usage with a current dictionary or a PLSI volume. |
| Bengali, Assamese, Odia | Biswas, Samsad Bengali-English Dictionary (on DSAL); Hemkosh (Assamese); Praharaj, Purnachandra Odia Bhashakosha (on DSAL) | |
| Marathi, Gujarati, Konkani | Molesworth, Marathi-English Dictionary (on DSAL); Date-Karve, Maharashtra Shabdakosha; Bhagavadgomandal (Gujarati); Konkani dictionaries from Goa University | |
| Punjabi, Sindhi, Kashmiri | Singh, The Panjabi Dictionary (1895, on DSAL); Punjabi University Patiala dictionaries; Grierson, A Dictionary of the Kashmiri Language (on DSAL) | |
| Northeast and tribal languages | People's Linguistic Survey of India volumes (peopleslinguisticsurvey.org; print, Orient BlackSwan); Linguistic Survey of India (Grierson, 1903 to 1928); university grammars; Ethnologue for classification only | Ethnologue is behind a paywall and is a classification, not an etymology source. For Bodo, Mizo, Khasi, Garo, Meitei, Nyishi and others, PLSI plus one academic grammar. |
| Who speaks what, how many | Census of India 2011, Table C-16 (Population by Mother Tongue) and C-17 (bilingualism), censusindia.gov.in; PLSI for languages the Census groups under "others" | The Census counts mother tongues as reported; it undercounts small languages and merges some. Say "Census 2011 counted" not "there are". Census 2021 data is not available as of this writing. |
| Place names | District gazetteers (state and British-era); Survey of India spellings; Census 2011 spellings; state government renaming orders for the date of a renaming; Hobson-Jobson for colonial forms; the above etymology sources for the words inside the name | A renaming order gives the date and the new name, never the meaning. |
| Script history | Salomon, Indian Epigraphy (OUP, 1998); Dani, Indian Palaeography (1963, 1986); Daniels and Bright, The World's Writing Systems (OUP, 1996); Unicode script proposals for modern letter inventories | |
| Slang and city speech | Published glossaries, newspaper features, novels and film scripts with the line quoted, linguistics papers on that city's speech; two independent attestations per word | Reddit and comment threads are leads, not sources. |

### 4.2 What is not a source

- Wikipedia and Wiktionary (leads only; follow their citations).
- Quora, Reddit, YouTube explainers, other Instagram pages.
- A single viewer's comment, however confident.
- "Everyone knows" and "it is well known that".
- Any AI-generated text, including this page's own drafts.

### 4.3 Grades of confidence on screen

| On-screen phrase | When to use it |
|---|---|
| "from" (no qualifier) | Two acceptable sources agree. |
| "probably from" | One acceptable source gives it with a qualifier, or sources differ on a detail but not the origin. |
| "disputed" with two readings shown | Acceptable sources give different origins. Both go on screen. |
| "the usual story, unproven" | A popular explanation that no acceptable source confirms. Say so; it is often the most rewatched beat. |

### 4.4 The three-region rule

From Module 3 section 7.1: every reel names three or more regions, or none. A reel about one state alone (a Sheher Ki Boli episode, a single Naam card) is fine, because it names no other region to compare against. A reel that compares Hindi with Tamil and stops there is not fine, because two-way comparisons read as a contest. Three or more makes it a map; two makes it a fight.

For Kya Kehte the floor is higher: at least one state from each of north, south, east, west and the Northeast on every map, or the map is not published. The Northeast is never cropped, never an inset, never "and others".

### 4.5 Script rule in brief

Hindi, Urdu, Punjabi, Marathi, Gujarati, Bengali and the rest appear in Roman script on screen and in captions by default (the project's standing rule and Module 1 section 4.1 on Hinglish). The native script is shown when the script is the point (Pillar 4), when a word's spelling in its own script carries the claim (a Tamil word with a retroflex the Roman form hides), and once in the header of a Sheher Ki Boli episode as a courtesy to the city. Transliteration follows one scheme per language, chosen for readability, not for ISO 15919 precision, and the scheme is written down in `09_brand.md`.

## 5. What we never post

- A reel that ranks languages, scripts or accents, or that implies one is older, purer, sweeter or harder than another. "Oldest language" claims in particular; the sources do not support a single answer and the comments become a war.
- A reel comparing exactly two regions or two languages.
- A map of India that is not the Survey of India outline, or that crops the Northeast, Lakshadweep or the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.
- An accent impression, a mocked pronunciation, or a "funny" reading of a regional English.
- Caste, religious or community slurs, even as "slang explained".
- Anything about the national language question, Hindi imposition, or state language policy as a political argument. The page shows how people speak; it does not take a side on how they should.
- An etymology that comes from a comment, a forward, a meme or memory without a source in section 4.
