# Module 9: Brand identity for the "how India speaks" page

Project Replay, module 9. Written 2026-11-06. Builds on Module 1 (sections 1 and 4), Module 2 (sections C and D), Module 3 (section 7) and Module 5 (pillars and sourcing). This is a new page, separate from AVRON and @marketkibatt; nothing here reuses their names, colours, type or sound.

Labels: [FACT] for a sourced finding, [INTERPRETATION] for my reading of it, [HYPOTHESIS] for an untested claim.

## 1. Name

### 1.1 What the name has to do

- Say "speech" or "words" in a way a Hindi speaker and an English speaker both get on first hearing.
- Be two syllables or three, spellable one way in Roman script, with no silent letters.
- Not be a word that a Tamil, Bengali or Malayali viewer would read as Hindi-first branding. Hindi words that have travelled into Indian English and into most regional registers are safer than literary Hindi.
- Not be an existing Indian brand, app, publisher or school in any adjacent field (language, education, media, personal care).
- Work as a handle with at most one suffix, and as a .in domain.

### 1.4 The pick: Bolchaal

Bolchaal means the way people actually talk, as opposed to the way the dictionary says they should, and that is the page in one word. It is a dictionary word, so nobody owns it, and the search found no Indian brand, app, school or page using it. It is spoken the same way across the Hindi belt, Maharashtra, Gujarat and Punjab, and an English speaker reads it right on first sight. It names the subject (speech) without naming a language, which matters for a page that must not read as Hindi-first in Chennai or Kolkata; the counter-argument is that it is still a Hindi-Urdu word, and the page answers that by making its second reel a Tamil one and by scheduling the Northeast in (section 7). It takes a bindu-style dot as a mark without strain (section 3.1). The handle @bolchaal cannot be verified from here; the owner should check it, then @bolchaal.in and @bolchaalpage, in that order.

Fallback one: Kya Kehte. It is the page's biggest series name (Module 5, pillar 1) and the question every reel asks. Weaker as a handle because the phrase is generic.

Fallback two: Wordmala. Bilingual, unclaimed, easy to say. Chosen only if both Hindi-word names fail the handle check, because it is the least serious of the three.

## 2. Visual identity

### 2.1 Stance

The page looks like a dictionary page that moves. Warm paper, ink type, one ochre accent, the India outline, the dictionary entry card. Nothing glossy, nothing gradient, no app chrome. It should look like the opposite of AVRON (bright white, Inter, indigo-violet-cyan-coral gradient, product UI) and the opposite of the exam-prep geography pages Module 3 section 7.2 warns about (dark blue, yellow headlines, satellite textures).

Why paper and not dark: a light warm frame reads as a card inside Instagram's dark UI and survives H.264 compression better than dark gradients, which band first (Module 7, section 2.6). Dark mode in the Instagram app changes the UI around the reel, not the reel, so the rule is that the frame must look deliberate against both black and white UI. Warm paper does; pure white does not (it blends into light mode and glares in dark mode).

### 2.2 Logo direction

Wordmark: "bolchaal" in lowercase, in the Latin display face (section 2.4), tracking slightly tight, with a single accent-colour dot after the final letter, raised to x-height. The dot is the mark.

Why a dot: almost every Indian script uses a dot diacritic. Devanagari anusvara and bindu, Tamil pulli, Kannada and Telugu sunna, Malayalam anusvaram, Gurmukhi bindi, Bengali and Gujarati anusvar. It is the one shape that belongs to all of them and to none. On the map it is the marker that lands on "your state". In the sound kit it is a click. It is the signature everyone will recognise in a muted feed. [INTERPRETATION]

Standalone mark (profile picture, cover corner): a single ochre dot on paper, with the lowercase "b" to its left when there is room. No speech bubble, no map silhouette, no quill, no globe, no mouth. No letter of any Indian script used as a logo, because that would pick a side.

Rules: the dot is always the accent colour, always a perfect circle, always the same size relative to the x-height (0.55 x-height), never animated except to land (one damped drop, 10 frames, no bounce).

Why Geru: red ochre is the colour of Warli walls, of temple floors in Tamil Nadu (kaavi), of Rajasthani thresholds, of a Bengali terracotta temple. It is pan-Indian and belongs to no party, no faith and no state flag. Saffron, green, and the blue of the Ashoka chakra were ruled out for the same reason in reverse: each is claimed. Geru also compresses well because it is a desaturated red; pure red fringes under 4:2:0 subsampling (Module 7, 2.6). The rule that it never appears as thin text on the dark base follows from the 3.3:1 contrast.

Dark mode and the grid: paper frames in a dark Instagram grid read as a stack of cards; the Syahi variant is kept for the Lipi series (ink on dark looks like chalk on slate and lets a stroke animation glow without a glow effect) and must never exceed two tiles in nine on the grid, so the grid stays recognisable (section 2.8).

Not allowed: gradients, a second accent, pure white, pure black, saffron, flag green, any blue, any violet or indigo (AVRON), coral or cyan (AVRON), neon, drop shadows, glow.

For maps that need more than five categories (a fifteen-name Kya Kehte), the rule is in section 2.5: categories are cognate families, five at most, and the label does the rest.

Sizes at 1080 by 1920: headword 120 to 160 px; map labels 44 to 56 px (Module 7, 3.9 says 44 px, four labels at once; Kya Kehte needs up to six, so the width axis is used, not a smaller size); body 40 px; source line 26 to 30 px mono; caption burn-ins 48 px. Nothing under 26 px on screen, ever.

Transliteration: one scheme per language, written for reading, not for ISO 15919. Long vowels doubled (aa, ee, oo), retroflex not marked, aspirates with h (kh, gh, th, dh), nasal ending as n. Tamil: zh for the retroflex approximant (Tamizh), because that is what Tamil speakers write. A one-page scheme per language goes in the brand folder before that language's first reel.

### 2.6 Motion signature: three moves on every reel

Every reel uses at least two of the three. Each has a sound twin (section 2.7).

1. The fill. One state at a time, 1.2 s per beat, the fill arrives as a hard-edged wipe from the state's centroid (no fade), the label snaps in at the end of the wipe, and the dot lands on the state with one damped drop. Cognate families fill together. Used in Kya Kehte, Aapne Bola, and as the opener for Naam.

2. The travelling word. The headword sits on a path (a map route or a timeline rule). It slides to the next hop, and during the slide its letters swap one at a time, left to right, into the next language's form: champo becomes shampoo in five letter swaps. The entry card for each hop rises under it. Used in Safar and Angrezi.

3. The seam. The last frame is built to be the first frame with one thing changed: one word on the object, one label on the map, one letter in the headword. The change lands on the final beat and the reel returns to its first frame with no cut. The viewer who loops sees the opening differently (Module 2, D1 reframe loop and E2 point 8). Used in Safar, Dhoondo, Kya Kehte, Lipi (where the stroke animation is itself the seam).

Supporting elements, not signature moves: the entry card (headword in serif, pronunciation, origin line, source line in mono, 1 px Patthar rule), the page-turn transition between cards (a hard cut with a 4-frame paper-slide and a foley), the stroke animation for Lipi (one letter drawn in hand order, 2 px ink line, 60 to 90 frames).

Banned: bouncy easing, camera shake, particles, glow, blur-in text, flashing, lens flares, 3D extrusion, any transition that is not a cut, a page-slide or the travelling word. Easing is one curve, cubic-bezier(0.6, 0, 0.2, 1), across the page.

### 2.7 Sound signature

Sonic logo. Three notes, played on a dry, pitched wooden mallet instrument (a sampled marimba or a tuned wood block, no reverb tail beyond 300 ms), contour rising then falling to mimic the spoken shape of "bol-chaal": G4, C5, A4, at 120 BPM, dotted rhythm (short, short, long). It plays once under the first frame and once, resolved on the final dot landing, at the seam. It must not be a sitar, a tabla, a flute or a conch, because those say "India" the way a tourist poster does. Key of G major, so it is not AVRON's D major kit. Generated once as a WAV from a real instrument sample, never synthesised in Python, and reused on every reel.

UI foley kit, one set made once and reused:

| Sound | Twin of | Direction for ElevenLabs sound generation |
|---|---|---|
| Pulli (the dot lands) | Logo dot, map marker | A single soft click, like a fingertip tapping thick paper, 80 ms, no pitch |
| Fill | The fill move | A short dry paper-brush sweep, 300 ms, one per state, slightly different pitch per family |
| Label snap | Label appears | A pencil tick on paper, 60 ms |
| Page turn | Card transition | One sheet of heavy paper turned, 350 ms, close-miked |
| Letter swap | The travelling word | A quiet wooden tick per letter, 40 ms, in rhythm |
| Stamp | Source line and verdict card | A rubber stamp on paper, 200 ms, used once per reel at most |
| Stroke | Lipi letter drawing | A nib on paper, continuous, 1 to 2 s, ducked under VO |
| Held silence | The beat before the outlier word | One beat of nothing (Module 2, D8); the frame holds too |

Density cap: at most one foley per beat and never two on the same frame; the kit sits at minus 20 LUFS short-term under VO at minus 16 (overall mix at minus 14 LUFS, Module 7 section 4.9).

Music: a single low paper-and-wood bed in G, 120 BPM, with no melody above the VO, used on Safar and Angrezi only; Kya Kehte, Naam and Dhoondo run on foley and VO alone, which makes them readable with the phone held to the ear (Module 1, 4.2 on the 45+ viewer).

VO direction for ElevenLabs. One voice, designed once with the voice design tool and saved, used on every reel: an Indian English accent of the kind heard on All India Radio English news and in metro newsrooms, not a specific city's; warm and even, as if explaining to a friend over tea, never a presenter's push; a gender-neutral note, meaning a mid-range voice that a listener does not immediately sort, or two saved voices (one lower, one higher) alternated by series, both with the same accent and pace; 150 to 165 words a minute; no uptalk; regional words spoken the way their speakers say them, so Tamil, Bengali, Malayalam and Punjabi words are read by a native-speaker voice clip where the design voice cannot, and the Hinglish lines are read as Hindi, not as English with Hindi words. Stability high, style low, so it does not act. Every regional-word pronunciation is checked by a native speaker before render, because a mispronounced Tamil word on a page about how India speaks is the one error the comments will never forgive.

## 3. Voice

### 3.1 Tone words

Curious. Plain. Sourced. Warm. Unbothered. Precise about what it does not know.

The page talks like the one friend in the group who actually looked it up, and says "I am not sure" when they are not.

### 3.2 Ten do and don't lines

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Lead with the word, then the place, then the fact | Lead with "did you know" |
| Say "probably", "disputed", "the usual story" when the source does | Say "actually" as if the viewer was wrong |
| Name the source in the sentence ("Hobson-Jobson has it in 1762") | Say "sources say" or "linguists say" |
| Use the Hinglish register of the viewer: Roman script, short clauses | Mix Hindi and English inside one on-screen line |
| Treat every language as equal: the same card, the same type size, the same care | Rank, compare two, or call any language old, pure, sweet or hard |
| Credit a correction by name and say what changed | Delete a correction, argue in the thread, or quietly re-upload |
| Write for the person who will forward it to a parent | Write for people who already know |
| End on the open question, the number, or the seam | End on "follow for more", "part 2 coming", "comment below" |
| Keep jokes in the situation, never in the pronunciation | Imitate an accent, in VO or in spelling ("vat is dis") |
| Say what the page is in one line when asked | Explain the page's mission in a caption |

### 3.3 Sample caption (Safar, "shampoo")

Shampoo is champo. Hindi "champna", to press, to knead. In 1762 Hobson-Jobson records "champo" as the massage that came with an Indian bath. Sake Dean Mahomed opened a "shampooing bath" in Brighton in 1814. By 1860 the English word meant washing hair, and by 1900 it was back in India on a bottle.

Hops and sources are on screen. The bottle line carries "probably": we have the word back in Indian English by 1900 but not a dated bottle.

Next Safar: where "loot" went.

Aapke yahan isse kya kehte hain?

(Illustrative. Before this caption ships, the 1762, 1814 and 1860 dates are checked against the OED and Hobson-Jobson entries for "shampoo", per Module 5 section 4.)

### 3.4 Sample comment reply

Commenter: "Wrong. In Kutch we say bhinda, not bhindi. Fix your map."

Reply: "Thanks, you are right for Kutchi. Bhagavadgomandal has bhinda for Kutchi and bhinda / bhindi for Gujarati. Our map showed only the Gujarati form. Sunday's Aapne Bola adds the Kutch label with your name on the credit, unless you would rather we did not. Tell us."

### 3.5 How to handle a correction in public

1. Own it, in the thread, within the day, from the page account. "You are right" or "we need to check this", nothing in between.
2. Cite. Name the source that settles it, or say the sources disagree and show both.
3. Thank, by name, and say where the credit will appear (Sunday's Aapne Bola).
4. Fix the asset: pin the correction comment, edit the caption with a dated "Correction:" line, and if the error is on screen in the first five seconds or in the reveal, replace the reel and say so. Never silently re-upload.
5. Log it in the claim registry with the date, so the same error is not made twice.

## 4. Editorial principles

1. Never rank languages, scripts, dialects or accents. No oldest, purest, sweetest, hardest, most scientific.
2. Never mock an accent or a pronunciation, in VO, in spelling, in a sound effect or in a caption.
3. Hindi, Urdu, Punjabi, Marathi, Gujarati, Bengali and all others in Roman script on screen and in captions by default, with one written transliteration scheme per language.
4. The native script is shown when the script is the point (Lipi), when the spelling carries the claim, and once as a header courtesy. When shown, it is set in the brand type at the same size and weight as the Latin, never as a decoration.
5. Every claim on screen carries a source line in mono, in the frame it is made, following the sourcing policy in Module 5 section 4. A reel that cannot carry its sources does not ship.
6. Three or more regions per reel, or none. Two is a contest. Kya Kehte maps show north, south, east, west and the Northeast on every map.
7. The Northeast, Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh, Goa, the islands, and the languages the Census groups as "others" are scheduled into the content calendar in advance (at least one of the seven weekly slots every week names one of them), not added when someone complains.
8. The map is always the Survey of India outline, whole, at one scale.
9. The page explains how people speak, not how they should. No position on language policy, the national language question, or script reform.
10. Faceless, and honest about it. No AI-generated presenter, no AI likeness of a real person, no celebrity, no film or song clip without licence, no synthesised SFX passed off as recorded.

If a name emerges by month three, use it once in an Aapne Bola credit and watch what happens. [HYPOTHESIS]

- Monday's question. Every Kya Kehte ends on "Aapke yahan?" with a blank tile. Same frame, same time, every week.
- Sunday's credits. Every Aapne Bola names the people who corrected or added. A credit is the page's only reward, and it is a real one; Module 1 section 3.4 on social currency applies.
- The open question. Every Sunday reel ends on one thing the page could not settle, and Friday's Sheher Ki Boli carries one "we are not sure" word. The page is visibly unfinished, on purpose.
- The weekly poll in Stories: one object, four names, "which one is yours", results shown Sunday. Instagram's poll sticker gives the count; the Story is also the quickest way to test an object before it earns a Monday map.

How comments become reels:

1. Every comment with a word in it is logged by hand into a sheet: word, place, language, commenter, date, source if given.
2. Three independent comments from one district for a word puts it on the "to verify" list; one acceptable source (Module 5, 4.1) moves it to a map.
3. The Sunday reel is cut from that sheet. The best three corrections get credits; the rest get a reply.
4. A question that appears ten times in a month becomes a Safar or Naam episode, with the first person who asked credited in the caption.
5. A region that has not appeared in four weeks goes to the top of the schedule, whatever the comments say.
