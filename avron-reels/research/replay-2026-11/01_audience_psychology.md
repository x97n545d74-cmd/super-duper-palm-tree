# Module 1: Indian Audience Psychological Blueprint

Project Replay, research module 1. Written 2026-11-06. Scope: why Indian Instagram users stop, watch, rewatch, share, save, comment, tag, visit, follow and bond with a reels page, and what to do about it.

Labels used throughout: [FACT: source] for a published finding or an official statement, [INTERPRETATION] for my reading of a fact, [HYPOTHESIS] for a claim with no direct evidence yet. Vendor-commissioned numbers (Meta, Kantar, Comscore, NapoleonCat) are marked as vendor data.

## 1. Summary

1. Instagram says the three signals that matter most for Reels ranking are watch time, likes and sends; sends matter slightly more for reaching non-followers. [FACT: Mosseri, Jan 2025]
2. Rewatching and sending are separate behaviours with separate causes: rewatching comes from missed detail or a desire to re-feel a peak; sending comes from the sender's relationship goals, not the reel's quality.
3. Curiosity is a felt gap between what the viewer knows and what they want to know. A hook has to open a gap the viewer already half-understands. [FACT: Loewenstein 1994]
4. High-arousal emotion (awe, anger, anxiety, amusement) drives sharing; sadness and calm do not. [FACT: Berger and Milkman 2012]
5. Memory of a reel is set by its peak and its ending, not its length. The last two seconds decide whether it is saved or sent. [FACT: Kahneman and colleagues 1993]
6. India is a WhatsApp-forward culture: Indians forward more than any other country, videos dominate what goes viral in groups, and roughly a quarter of viral content in rural groups is misinformation. Trust is the scarce asset. [FACT: WhatsApp 2018; arXiv 2407.08172]
7. 98% of Indian internet users access content in an Indian language; Hinglish is the default register for young urban users on social platforms. [FACT: IAMAI-Kantar ICUBE 2024; Sengupta et al 2024]
8. Instagram reaches 71% of 15 to 24 year olds and 65% of 25 to 34 year olds in India; Facebook leads above that. The 45+ audience arrives through family forwards, not the Reels tab. [FACT: Comscore 2025, vendor data] [INTERPRETATION]
9. Cricket and festivals are the shared emotional calendar: they give a page predictable windows of collective pride and nostalgia.
10. Most Indian-specific psychology evidence is thin or vendor-made. This document separates the two and lists the gaps for the test plan.

## 2. Twelve behaviours and why they happen

Each behaviour has its own cause. They are not stages of one funnel; a viewer can save without finishing or send without liking.

| Behaviour | Distinct psychological explanation | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Stop scrolling | An orienting response: an automatic shift of attention to a change in the stimulus (new face, motion onset, cut, sound onset). It is involuntary and happens before any judgement of quality. The viewer stops because the brain has already allocated resources. | [FACT: Lang 2000, Journal of Communication 50, 46-70] |
| 2. Become curious | An information gap: the viewer notices a specific thing they do not know but feel they should. Curiosity needs some prior knowledge; a total unknown creates no itch. Reward circuitry (caudate) activates while the gap is open, which is why the open question feels pleasant as well as tense. | [FACT: Loewenstein 1994; Kang et al 2009] |
| 3. Watch to the end | Anticipation of a resolution. Dopamine neurons fire on the prediction of reward, not only on the reward. A reel that keeps a promised payoff pending (and signals progress toward it) keeps the viewer. Each related cut refreshes attention without costing processing capacity. | [FACT: Schultz, Dayan and Montague 1997; Lang et al 1993] |
| 4. Rewatch | There are two separate causes. (a) Missed detail: the viewer suspects the reel held more than one pass caught (a fast number, a hidden visual joke, a line in the background). (b) Re-consumption for emotional regulation: people replay things to re-feel a known peak in a predictable way. Both are strengthened when the end loops seamlessly into the start, so the second viewing starts before a decision is made. | [FACT: Russell and Levy 2012] [INTERPRETATION for the loop] |
| 5. Share with a friend | Driven by the sender's goal rather than by the reel. Sharing serves impression management ("I know good stuff"), emotion regulation ("I need to laugh with someone about this"), social bonding ("this is our thing") and persuasion. The sender asks "what does sending this say about me to this person?" | [FACT: Berger 2014, Journal of Consumer Psychology] |
| 6. Save | Future-self planning. A save is a promise to return, made when the reel contains usable information the viewer cannot absorb now (a list, a formula, a step sequence). Saves are private, so they are not driven by status. No peer-reviewed study of Instagram saves was found. | [HYPOTHESIS, consistent with Instagram's own description of saves as a private signal] |
| 7. Comment | Self-disclosure is intrinsically rewarding: people give up money to share their own opinions, and disclosure activates reward circuitry. Comments happen when the reel gives the viewer a chance to state a position (agree, disagree, add their own case) with low effort. | [FACT: Tamir and Mitchell 2012, PNAS] |
| 8. Tag someone | Relationship maintenance in public. In a large Instagram sample, 54.8% of comments contained a user tag, and about 48% of tagged comments were relationship-oriented ("this is you", "us next weekend") versus 44% information-oriented. A tag is a lightweight way to say "I thought of you" where others can see it. | [FACT: Kang, Yoon and Han 2020 and 2022] |
| 9. Visit profile | A trust check. After a surprising claim or an unusual style, the viewer asks "who is this and are they legitimate?" Profile visits are a verification behaviour, so they spike on claims that are credible enough to check and surprising enough to need checking. | [INTERPRETATION] |
| 10. Follow | A bet on future value plus the start of a parasocial bond. People follow when they expect more of what they just got (predictable format) and when the page feels like a person with a consistent voice. Perceived authenticity, reciprocity and intimacy strengthen parasocial bonds. | [FACT: parasocial literature, e.g. Acta Psychologica 2022 study on influencer credibility] [INTERPRETATION for format predictability] |
| 11. Emotional connection with a page | Narrative transportation plus repeated exposure. When a page's reels pull the viewer into a story world with a recognisable voice, the viewer's beliefs shift and they identify with the page's stance. Repetition of one look and one sound builds familiarity, which reads as trust. | [FACT: Green and Brock 2000] [INTERPRETATION for brand repetition] |
| 12. Send in a WhatsApp group | Group identity work. A group send is a public act within a tribe (family, batchmates, office). The sender chooses content that confirms the group's shared identity, gives the group something to react to, or lets the sender play a role (the news-bringer, the joker, the elder who warns). It is forwarded, not authored, which lowers the sender's risk. | [FACT: Berger 2014 on social bonding; Malhotra 2023 on family groups as mixed news and interpersonal spaces] [INTERPRETATION] |

## 3. Seven mechanisms

### 3.1 Curiosity and information gaps

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Scientific explanation | Curiosity is a deprivation state that appears when attention lands on a gap between what one knows and what one wants to know. The gap has to be specific and partly known; a question about something the viewer has never heard of produces no curiosity. Curiosity also improves memory for the answer, especially when the answer is surprising. |
| Relevant research | Loewenstein, G. (1994). The psychology of curiosity. Psychological Bulletin 116(1), 75-98. https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/ . Kang, M. J. et al. (2009). The wick in the candle of learning. Psychological Science 20(8). https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf |
| Indian cultural application | Indian viewers carry a dense shared knowledge base (cricket records, Bollywood, school-exam facts, prices of everyday things, family rituals). A gap works best when it sits inside that base: "the one rule in your PPF account nobody reads" lands; "the Basel III capital rule" does not, because there is no prior knowledge to be deprived of. |
| Reel application | Open with a specific, partly known fact and withhold one piece. Show the viewer that they almost know the answer (a familiar object, a familiar number) before the question. Pay the gap off, and make the payoff surprising, because surprise is what gets remembered. |
| Effective execution | First frame: a Zomato bill, blurred total, text "ye ek line tumhara 2,400 rupaye le gayi" (this one line took 2,400 rupees from you). The line is revealed at 0:04, the mechanism at 0:12, the fix at 0:20. The viewer knows what a food bill is, so the gap is small and sharp. |
| Bad execution | "You won't believe what this company did" over a logo the viewer does not know. No prior knowledge, no gap, no reason to stay. Or a gap that is opened and never closed (clickbait), which trains the audience to distrust the page. |
| Metric most influenced | Watch time first, then saves when the payoff is usable. |

### 3.2 Pattern interruption

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Scientific explanation | Structural features of video (cuts, motion onset, sound onset, new voices) trigger an orienting response, an automatic attention shift that does not habituate with repetition. Related cuts (same scene, continued meaning) refresh attention at low processing cost; unrelated cuts grab attention but cost capacity and hurt memory for what follows. A reel is one long sequence of orienting triggers; the design question is how often and how related. |
| Relevant research | Lang, A. (2000). The limited capacity model of mediated message processing. Journal of Communication 50(1), 46-70. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP . Lang, A., Geiger, S., Strickwerda, M. and Sumner, J. (1993). The effects of related and unrelated cuts on television viewers' attention, processing capacity, and memory. Communication Research 20(1), 4-29. https://scilit.com/publications/9e903f69b2eb53c627dfcd43c49c48f2 |
| Indian cultural application | Indian feeds are visually loud (text-heavy, saturated, many faces). The interruption that stops an Indian scroller is often quiet and clean rather than louder: a plain white frame, one number, silence before a sound. No Indian study on this was found; the claim is an interpretation of the general finding that contrast with the surrounding stream drives the orienting response. |
| Reel application | Place one interruption every 2 to 4 seconds, and make most of them related cuts (same scene, new element) so attention refreshes without a memory cost. Reserve one unrelated cut for the turn of the story. Sound onsets count: a single UI click on a black frame is an interruption. |
| Effective execution | A finance explainer in which each new number arrives as a related cut (same panel, new row) every 3 seconds, with one hard cut to a full-screen receipt at the turning point. The viewer's attention is refreshed ten times, and the one big cut lands where the memory should form. |
| Bad execution | A cut every 0.5 seconds with unrelated stock footage, zoom punches and three sound effects at once. Attention is captured and then wasted; recall of the content drops. The viewer feels assaulted and swipes. |
| Metric most influenced | Watch time (the first 3 seconds specifically). Secondary: rewatch when a related-cut detail was missed. |

### 3.3 Emotional contagion

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Scientific explanation | People automatically mimic and synchronise with the expressions, voices and postures of others and then converge emotionally. Contagion does not need a face or voice: text alone spread emotion across 689,003 Facebook users in a controlled experiment, though the effect size was small. For sharing, the emotion's arousal level matters more than its valence: awe, anger, anxiety and amusement spread; sadness and contentment do not. |
| Relevant research | Hatfield, E., Cacioppo, J. and Rapson, R. (1993). Emotional contagion. Current Directions in Psychological Science 2(3). Kramer, A., Guillory, J. and Hancock, J. (2014). Experimental evidence of massive-scale emotional contagion. PNAS 111(24). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4066473/ . Berger, J. and Milkman, K. (2012). What makes online content viral? Journal of Marketing Research 49(2), 192-205. https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf |
| Indian cultural application | Indian audiences respond strongly to collective-pride stimuli (ISRO landings, cricket wins, a kirana shop beating a chain) and to righteous anger about being cheated (hidden charges, mis-sold insurance). Both are high-arousal. Sentimental sadness ("poor farmer" montages) gets likes but not sends. No Indian experimental evidence found; the pattern is read from Berger and Milkman plus Indian viral examples. [INTERPRETATION] |
| Reel application | Pick one high-arousal emotion per reel and commit. For a graphics-only page the carrier is voice tone, pacing and the moment of reveal, not a face. The VO's breath and tempo transmit arousal; a flat read kills contagion even with a strong fact. |
| Effective execution | A reel that builds controlled anger: a bank statement, a fee line appearing one at a time with a soft tick, then the annual total. The viewer's jaw tightens with each tick. The payoff is a legal way to get the fee reversed, which turns anger into useful energy and makes the send feel like helping. |
| Bad execution | A sad violin montage about job losses with a slow VO. Low-arousal sadness, no action, no reason to send. Or forced hype ("INSANE, you NEED to see this") that signals arousal without earning it. |
| Metric most influenced | Sends and comments (anger and awe), likes (awe). |

### 3.4 Identity and social currency

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Scientific explanation | Word of mouth is goal-driven. Senders use content to manage impressions (look smart, in the know, caring), to regulate their own emotions, to bond, to gather information and to persuade. Self-disclosure is itself rewarding; people pay to share their opinions. So a reel gets shared when sharing it lets the sender say something about themselves cheaply. |
| Relevant research | Berger, J. (2014). Word of mouth and interpersonal communication: a review and directions for future research. Journal of Consumer Psychology 24(4). https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/WOM-Annual-Rev-of-Psych.pdf (2024 Annual Review follow-up by the same author). Tamir, D. and Mitchell, J. (2012). Disclosing information about the self is intrinsically rewarding. PNAS 109(21). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3361411/ |
| Indian cultural application | Indian identity on social media is layered: family role, regional language, college, city, and class signals (NCCS A and B). A reel that lets a viewer perform "the sensible one in the family" (money warnings), "the proud Tamilian / Punjabi" (regional pride), or "the person who gets the joke" (insider Hinglish humour) is socially spendable. Hinglish itself is an identity marker: on Twitter, over a third of Hindi-English bilingual users preferred Hinglish to connect with followers. [FACT: Sengupta et al 2024, vendor-free academic study] |
| Reel application | Design the reel around the sentence the sender will type with it: "dekh, maine bola tha" (see, I told you), "ye tu hai" (this is you), "bhai ye sahi hai" (this is correct). If no such sentence exists, the reel will be liked, not sent. |
| Effective execution | A reel that ends with a checklist of three things "people who understand money do in October". The sender forwards it to a sibling with "tu 2 kar chuka hai" (you have done 2). The reel supplied the identity ("people who understand money") and the sender claimed it. |
| Bad execution | A reel whose only possible caption is "nice video". It can be good and still not move, because it gives the sender nothing to say about themselves. |
| Metric most influenced | Sends, tags, comments. |

### 3.5 Narrative transportation

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Scientific explanation | Transportation is absorption into a story: imagery, feeling and attention all pulled into the narrative world. Transported readers change beliefs in the direction of the story and are less likely to counter-argue. The effect held whether the story was labelled fact or fiction. For a 30 to 60 second reel, transportation means the viewer stops evaluating the reel and starts living the sequence. |
| Relevant research | Green, M. C. and Brock, T. C. (2000). The role of transportation in the persuasiveness of public narratives. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 79(5), 701-721. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5267340 (review context). Green and Appel (2024) preprint update: https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf |
| Indian cultural application | Indian storytelling convention is character-first and consequence-heavy (the film interval, the serial cliffhanger, the "phir kya hua" (then what happened) of oral storytelling). A finance reel transports when it follows one named person through one decision with a visible consequence, not when it lists facts. The person can be a type ("a 27-year-old in Pune with her first salary") rather than a real individual, which also keeps the page clear of likeness problems. |
| Reel application | One protagonist, one decision, one consequence, one turn. Keep the camera language consistent so the viewer never leaves the world to decode the format. Give time markers ("March", "6 months later") so the viewer's imagination fills in the gap. |
| Effective execution | "Priya ne 2019 mein 50,000 ka SIP shuru kiya" (Priya started a 50,000 rupee SIP in 2019). Her app screen is the world. We watch 2020 fall, her thumb hover over "redeem", and the 2024 screen. The viewer is inside Priya's screen, so the lesson about staying invested lands as her relief, not as advice. |
| Bad execution | "5 mistakes investors make" as a numbered list with no person and no time. It is correct and forgettable, and it transports nobody. The viewer evaluates each item instead of living any of them. |
| Metric most influenced | Watch time and follows (people follow a world they want to return to). Secondary: emotional connection with the page. |

### 3.6 Memory and anticipation

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Scientific explanation | Two findings. First, retrospective evaluation of an experience is predicted by its peak and its end, with duration mostly ignored (the peak-end rule and duration neglect). Second, dopamine neurons fire to the prediction of a reward and fire harder when the reward beats the prediction; a fully predicted reward produces little signal. For a reel this means: the viewer's memory of the reel is its best moment and its last two seconds, and the pleasure of watching comes from a payoff that arrives slightly better or sooner than expected. |
| Relevant research | Kahneman, D., Fredrickson, B., Schreiber, C. and Redelmeier, D. (1993). When more pain is preferred to less. Psychological Science 4(6). https://ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:5ae9adc9-61ec-4174-b37c-4b752f36c23b/Kahnemann%20et%20al.%20-%20When%20More%20Pain%20is%20Preferred%20to%20Less%20(1993).pdf . Fredrickson, B. and Kahneman, D. (1993). Duration neglect in retrospective evaluations. JPSP 65(1), 45-55. Schultz, W., Dayan, P. and Montague, P. R. (1997). A neural substrate of prediction and reward. Science 275(5306). https://gruber.yale.edu/recipient/wolfram-schultz . Russell, C. and Levy, S. (2012). The temporal and focal dynamics of volitional reconsumption. Journal of Consumer Research 39(2). https://www.newswise.com/articles/the-science-of-re-runs-why-we-watch-our-favorite-episode-of-a-tv-show-or-listen-to-a-favorite-song-over-and-over-again |
| Indian cultural application | The Indian viewer has a trained expectation of the "twist" from film and serial culture, and of the "moral" from school and family storytelling. A reel whose end is the moral gets remembered as a lesson; a reel whose end is the twist gets remembered as an experience and rewatched. Indian re-consumption of known media is documented: during the 2020 lockdown, Doordarshan re-telecasts of Ramayan and Mahabharat drew a viewership surge and about 64% positive sentiment in one analysis. [FACT: BARC via Ranjan 2020, arXiv 2101.02628] |
| Reel application | Put the strongest moment at roughly two-thirds, then end on a second, smaller peak that loops into the opening frame. Never end on a logo, a disclaimer or a slow fade; that becomes the memory. Beat the expectation once: deliver the payoff a beat earlier than the viewer predicted or with one extra detail they did not ask for. |
| Effective execution | A reel whose last frame is the same receipt as its first frame, now with one line highlighted. The viewer, already thinking "wait, that was the first frame", is three seconds into the second viewing before deciding to rewatch. The peak (the highlighted line) and the end are the same moment. |
| Bad execution | A strong middle followed by 8 seconds of "follow for more, link in bio, not financial advice". Peak-end rule says the memory is now the disclaimer. Or a payoff fully telegraphed at 0:03 so the reveal at 0:25 produces no prediction error. |
| Metric most influenced | Rewatch and saves. Secondary: follows (the memory of the page is the memory of its endings). |

### 3.7 Social relationships: who sends what to whom

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Scientific explanation | Sharing is channel- and audience-specific. The same person sends different content to a best friend, a partner, a parent, a college group and an office group because each send does a different social job: bonding, care, status, coordination or play. Family WhatsApp groups in India mix interpersonal talk with news and misinformation; young adults see older relatives as more gullible and usually ignore bad forwards to avoid conflict. Indian users forward more messages, photos and videos than any other country, and in rural private groups, videos made up over half of viral messages despite being about 3% of all content (first version of the study). |
| Relevant research | Berger (2014) as above. Malhotra, P. (2023). Misinformation in WhatsApp family groups. Digital Journalism 12(5). https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21670811.2023.2213731 . Global patterns of viral content on WhatsApp (2024-25, ICWSM). https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08172 . WhatsApp forward limits announcement (2018), reported at https://mobilemarketingmagazine.com/whatsapp-restricts-message-forwarding-on-the-back-of-mob-killings-in-india/ . Kang, Yoon and Han (2020) on tagging: https://ji1kang.github.io/resources/www20companion-46.pdf |
| Indian cultural application | See the table below. The key Indian feature is that the family group is a cross-generational broadcast channel with low trust in forwards (45% of people in tier-2 and tier-3 cities said they never believe WhatsApp forwards in a 2019 survey reported by Business Standard, vendor survey). A finance page therefore wins group sends by being visibly sourced, calm and non-partisan, so the sender is not embarrassed. |
| Reel application | Decide the target relationship before writing. A best-friend send and a family-group send need different endings: the first ends on a joke or a "ye tu hai" moment, the second on a sourced, screenshot-able fact. |
| Effective execution | A reel about a new tax rule that ends on one clean frame: rule, date, source, in large type. It is forwarded to family groups because the frame survives as a still even when nobody plays the video. |
| Bad execution | A reel that is correct but delivered with sarcasm about "uncles". It will be sent between friends and never into the family group it is about. |
| Metric most influenced | Sends (DM and WhatsApp), tags. |

## 4. Indian-specific findings

### 4.1 Language

- 886 million active internet users in 2024, 55% rural; 98% accessed content in an Indian language. [FACT: IAMAI-Kantar ICUBE 2024, vendor report] https://www.iamai.in/sites/default/files/research/Kantar_%20IAMAI%20report_2024_.pdf
- Preference for Indian-language content is higher outside metros (53%) than in metros (39%). [FACT: Mindshare-e4m 2024, vendor report] https://www.exchange4media.com/digital-news/93-youtube-viewers-watch-indian-language-content-mindshare-e4m-content-marketing-report-134060.html
- Hinglish: analysis of 260,000 X posts by 17,000 users found over a third preferred Hinglish to connect with followers; political posts were the most code-mixed, then Bollywood and sports; Hindi is seldom written in Devanagari on social media. [FACT: Sengupta et al 2024, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications; Rudra et al 2016, EMNLP] https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-03058-6.pdf
- Tamil, Telugu and Kannada are the key languages after Hindi and English for micro-drama discovery via social feeds. [FACT: Meta-Ormax 2026, vendor report] https://theprint.in/economy/social-feeds-power-indias-micro-drama-boom-meta-ormax-report/2882528
- Bengali, Gujarati and Punjabi content were growing in triple digits on YouTube (2024). Punjabi was the most chosen regional language in one 2022 consumer survey (24.3%), Marathi 9.9%. [FACT: Mindshare-e4m 2024; Iplix 2022, both vendor]
- Working interpretation for a Hinglish page: English for the on-screen numbers and sources (credibility), Hindi in Roman script for the emotional and relational lines (identity), and the two never in the same sentence on screen. [INTERPRETATION] A later regional pass (Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Bengali, Punjabi) should be dubbed VO plus translated end frame, not a new page, because the end frame is what gets forwarded. [HYPOTHESIS]

### 4.2 Generations

- Instagram reach in India: 71% of 15 to 24, 65% of 25 to 34; YouTube above 85% in both; Facebook leads older groups (76% of 25 to 34 and ahead of Instagram above 35). Social platforms reach 89.3% of the digital population. [FACT: Comscore State of Social India 2025, vendor] https://marketingmind.in/india-spends-twenty-hrs-a-month-on-social-media-youtube-leads-as-top-platform-comscore
- Instagram's Indian audience is roughly 67 to 70% male and the 18 to 24 band is the largest. [FACT: NapoleonCat 2025 estimates, vendor] https://napoleoncat.com/stats/instagram-users-in-india/2025/02
- About 500 million social media users spent roughly 880 billion hours in 2025; 46% of Instagram consumption is short video. [FACT: FICCI-EY 2025, vendor] https://storyboard18.com/social-media/indias-social-media-user-base-reaches-500-million-in-2025-users-clock-880-billion-hours-93071.htm
- Reading by band. 16 to 24: lives in the Reels tab, sends in DMs, speaks Hinglish, high meme literacy, low trust in finance pages. 25 to 34: the largest money-decision group (first salary, first SIP, first loan), still Instagram-native, the sender into family groups. 35 to 44: Facebook and WhatsApp heavier, arrives at reels through forwards, values sourced and calm content. 45+: reaches reels almost only via WhatsApp forwards from children; a reel for them must work as a still frame and as audio with the phone held to the ear. [INTERPRETATION built on the Comscore reach figures and Malhotra 2023; the behaviour per band is not measured]

### 4.3 WhatsApp-forward culture

- WhatsApp stated in 2018 that Indians forward more messages, photos and videos than any other country and limited forwards in India first. [FACT: WhatsApp 2018]
- In rural private groups, political, activist and caste-based groups drove the most viral content; over 25% of viral content in India was misinformation, much of it religious. [FACT: arXiv 2407.08172]
- Family groups: 26 Delhi young adults described them as mixed spaces of chat and news; they see elders as more gullible and rarely correct them. [FACT: Malhotra 2023]
- 45% of people in smaller cities said they never believe forwards. [FACT: 2019 survey via Business Standard, vendor]
- Meta: messaging is the most popular way to share photos and videos on Instagram; Reels are reshared over 4.5 billion times a day across Meta apps (global figure); Instagram is testing a Reels-first layout in India with DMs one swipe away. [FACT: Meta newsroom, Sept 2025] https://about.fb.com/news/2025/09/in-india-instagram-debuts-a-reels-first-experience-for-its-mobile-app/
- Implication: the forward environment is high-volume and low-trust. A page that is consistently sourced becomes the thing people forward when they want to be believed. The forwardable unit is the final still frame and the first 3 seconds of audio, because many recipients never press play. [INTERPRETATION]

### 4.4 Family and group viewing

- Indian family groups function as cross-generational broadcast channels (above). No study of co-viewing of reels on one phone was found. [HYPOTHESIS: a meaningful share of 45+ viewing happens on a child's or spouse's phone, so the reel must be legible at arm's length and without earphones]
- Meta's own festive research says 65% of festive shoppers engaged with Meta apps and that Reels were among the most influential formats; 82% of surveyed people had followed a business after watching Reels (Factworks via Meta). [FACT: Meta/Ipsos 2025 and Meta/Factworks 2024, vendor]

### 4.5 Festivals and cricket as the emotional calendar

- The IPL 2026 opener drew 448 million Instagram views; content creation spikes during match hours; IPL 2024 content had an engagement rate of 2.89 versus about 1.2 for the T20 World Cup. [FACT: third-party analyst reports via Storyboard18 and Social Samosa, vendor]
- Cricket and Indian national identity are linked in the academic literature; Twitter discourse during the 2019 World Cup flagged the nation as much as it discussed players. [FACT: Nair 2011, Sport in Society 14(5); ECPR 2019 conference paper]
- Meta's festive reports place Diwali shopping and Reels together every year since 2022. [FACT: Meta, vendor]

### 4.6 Pride, awe and nostalgia triggers

- A culture-specific nostalgia scale for India (seven studies, 1,823 respondents) found a distinct "collective nostalgia" dimension alongside personal past imagery. - Nostalgic media engagement among Indian Gen Y increased social sharing, moderated by social connectedness. [FACT: Organizations and Markets in Emerging Economies study] https://www.journals.vu.lt/omee/article/view/42736/41022
- ISRO's 2019 request for childhood moon memories produced a visible nostalgia wave; the 2023 landing was widely described as collective pride. [FACT: press reporting, not a study]
- For finance: pride works when a familiar Indian company, city or person wins on the world stage; awe works on scale comparisons (one number next to a familiar object); nostalgia works on prices and products from the viewer's childhood (the price of a Parle-G packet, Doordarshan, the first mobile recharge).
